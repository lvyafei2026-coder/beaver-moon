Watching the November Full Moon: Supermoons, Timing, and Direction

A full moon in November is simple to plan around once you know three things: whether the full phase lands near the Moon's closest approach to Earth that year, when in the month it falls, and where it will come up over your horizon. The lunar cycle doesn't line up with the calendar, so the date slides from year to year and the supermoon label comes and goes. Here is how to read both, and when to be outside.

Why November's Full Moon Climbs So High

A full moon always sits opposite the Sun in the sky, so its height above the horizon is the mirror image of the Sun's position six months away. In November the Sun rides low across the northern sky, which pushes the full moon correspondingly high. At mid-northern latitudes it can pass close to overhead around midnight, higher than at almost any other full moon of the year.

That altitude changes what you see. Less atmosphere between you and the Moon means steadier, whiter light and sharper crater detail through binoculars, and the Moon stays up for most of the long November night. Observers in the Southern Hemisphere get the reverse: the November full moon stays low and skims the northern horizon, which makes it dimmer and more colourful but harder to see from a built-up area.

Supermoon, or Just a Full Moon?

The Moon's orbit is an ellipse, so its distance varies through the month. Perigee, the closest point, is roughly 363,000 km away; apogee, the farthest, around 405,000 km; the average is about 384,400 km. When the full phase happens near perigee, the Moon is both full and unusually close, and that combination is what people mean by a supermoon. There is no single official definition, but the common working rule is a full moon within about 90% of its closest approach.

A supermoon is often quoted as appearing around 14% wider and 30% brighter than a full moon at apogee. Those numbers are real, but comparisons are always against the farthest case. Next to an ordinary full moon the difference is modest and easy to miss without photographs taken at the same scale. The bigger visual effect on most nights is the Moon illusion, which makes a low Moon look enormous near the horizon.

Not every November full moon is a supermoon. It depends purely on where the full phase falls in the orbit that year: some years the November full moon is an unremarkable average distance, and in other years it lands close enough to be among the nearest full moons of the year. The label is calendar luck, not a property of November itself.

The Date Slides, and Sometimes Doubles

A lunar month runs about 29.5 days, while calendar months run 30 or 31, and the solar year is roughly 11 days longer than twelve lunar months. The practical result is that a November full moon typically falls around ten days earlier than it did the year before, then jumps later when an extra full moon resets the pattern. Over 19 years the dates roughly repeat, a cycle astronomers have used for centuries.

Because November has 30 days and the lunar cycle is a little shorter, a November with two full moons is possible, one at the very start of the month and one at the very end. It is uncommon but not remarkable — the same arithmetic allows a double full moon in any 30-day month. In those years the earlier one is the one most people notice, and the second is the one that gets called a blue moon.

For planning purposes, treat the date as movable and check it for the specific year you care about rather than assuming a mid-month slot. The full phase also lasts only an instant in clock terms; the Moon looks full to the eye for roughly a night on either side of that moment, which gives you some slack if the weather turns.

Best Hour to Watch, and Where to Look

A full moon rises about the time the Sun sets, because the two are on opposite sides of the sky. In November, northern observers see it come up in the east, slightly north of due east, and set in the west, slightly north of due west; southern observers see it rise farther south. The exact rise time shifts with latitude and date, so a local moonrise time for your position is worth checking rather than relying on a national figure.

The best hour is usually the first one after moonrise. The Moon is low, so you get the horizon as a size reference, and light travels through more atmosphere, which warms it to yellow or orange. Later, near the highest point of its arc, it is smaller-looking, brilliant white, and often uncomfortably bright through any optics.

You need nothing to see it. Binoculars — 7x50 or 10x50 — are the sweet spot for beginners and show the dark plains and the bright crater rays clearly. A small telescope is fine at low to medium magnification; at high power the full Moon is dazzling and a moon filter helps. Give your eyes ten minutes away from streetlights and the view improves noticeably.

Weather and Sky Conditions That Decide the View

In many temperate regions November is a cloudy month, and the forecast that matters is not the overall one but the sky along the horizon where the Moon comes up. A mostly clear sky with a bank of cloud low in the east will hide the moonrise and then deliver a sharp Moon an hour later. Haze near the horizon dims and reddens a rising Moon: sometimes it ruins the view, sometimes it makes the photograph.

Light pollution barely affects the Moon, which is bright enough to cut through urban skyglow — from a city centre it is often the only astronomical object you can see. What does suffer is your night vision, so find a spot shielded from direct streetlights and give your eyes time to adapt. Cold, clear nights are the best ones for detail, but they also fog binocular lenses and telescope optics, so let equipment cool outside before use and keep eyepieces in a pocket.

What to Look for on the Surface

A full moon is the hardest phase for surface detail, because sunlight falls straight on and casts almost no shadows. The dark maria — the large basaltic plains — are still obvious and form the familiar pattern most people learn first. The bright rays radiating from craters such as Tycho are at their most visible now, since they are made of reflective ejecta that shows up best under direct lighting.

If you want relief and depth, look along the edge of the disc. Right at the limb, shadows are long even at full phase, and mountain ranges and crater rims stand out as ragged notches. Watching over two or three hours shows the terminator creeping, which is the clearest demonstration that you are looking at a rotating sphere lit from one side.

Frequently asked questions

Is the November full moon always a supermoon?

No. It depends on whether the full phase happens near the Moon's closest point to Earth that year. Some years it is a fairly average full moon, and in other years it is one of the closest of the year.

What time does the November full moon rise?

Roughly around sunset, because a full moon sits opposite the Sun. The exact time varies with your latitude and the date, so check a local moonrise time for your position.

Does a supermoon really look bigger?

It is genuinely a few percent wider and brighter than an average full moon, but the difference is hard to see without comparing photographs. A low Moon near the horizon looks large mostly because of the Moon illusion, which is a perceptual effect.

Can I see the November full moon from the Southern Hemisphere?

Yes. It is the same Moon, but it stays lower in the sky and appears upside down compared with the northern view, so the familiar maria pattern is inverted.

Do I need a telescope?

No. The full moon is the easiest object in the sky to observe with the naked eye, and ordinary binoculars will show the dark plains and the brightest crater rays.
